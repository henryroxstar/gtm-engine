# What the best inside sales teams do differently when drafting a cold email

**Date:** 2026-08-26 · **Type:** research brief / evidence base
**Question answered:** for a first-touch cold email to a CxO in the **US** and in **Singapore** — what
structure, elements, length, tone, and CTA does the evidence actually support, what are the pitfalls,
and what do elite teams do that average teams don't?

**Deliberately out of scope** (you already have these): prospect research and qualification;
deliverability and spam filtering ([`email-deliverability.md`](email-deliverability.md)); legal
compliance ([`email-compliance.md`](email-compliance.md)).

**Relationship to the rest of the docs.** [`email-optimization.md`](email-optimization.md) remains the
**owner of the prescriptive craft floor** — the rules this repo actually enforces. This file is the
**external evidence base** behind those rules plus one thing nothing else owns: the **US vs. Singapore
CxO register analysis** (§6). Where this file and the playbook disagree, the playbook wins for
operations; this file wins for "what is the evidence, and how good is it."

> **Every example name, person and company in this document is fictional.** Real named individuals
> appear only as *citations of their published work* (Braun, Allen-Knuth, Meyer, Bohns, Riesterer,
> Allred, Bay), never as example data.

document as a **direction with a magnitude you should not quote in a deck.** The rules survive; the
decimals do not.

## 1. The ten-line answer

If you read nothing else:

1. **The email is an output of research, not of writing.** Signal-anchored emails run 5–18% reply vs 1–3% for generic (grade E/B); Salesloft's 3.4M-cadence sample puts personalisation past a 15% threshold at **2.75× the reply rate** of zero personalisation.
2. **One idea. One reason you wrote today. One ask.** A CxO gives it about **nine seconds**.
3. **50–100 words for a CxO first touch.** Never past ~125. Reply rates "drop sharply" past 100 (Gong).
4. **Subject line: 1–5 words, lowercase, their world not your product.** Optimise it for *reply*, not open — opens are no longer measurable honestly.
5. **Do not pitch in touch one.** Pitching is the single most damaging measured behaviour: **up to −57% reply rate** (Gong, 28M+ emails).
6. **Ask for interest, not for time.** The best-evidenced craft rule in the whole field (Gong, 304,174 emails) — and it has an independent academic mechanism (§5.6).
7. **Write at a grade 3–5 reading level.** Boomerang: 3rd-grade level got **+36%** response vs college level (5.3M messages).
8. **Give an easy, costless "no."** It raises reply rate and it is the only way to get truthful negatives out of a high-face culture (§6.3).
9. **Tone is register, not word count.** For a Singapore CxO you change *formality, name handling, credibility source and forwardability* — not length.
10. **Measure replies and meetings. Never opens.**

## 2. What elite teams actually do differently

This is the part most "cold email tips" articles miss. The gap between a 2% team and a 10% team is
mostly **process**, not prose. Gong's analysis of 28M+ cold emails found top reps book **8.1× more
meetings** and get **4.2× more replies** than average reps — while the average rep must send **344
emails to land one meeting** ([Gong, "Does cold email even work any more?"](https://www.gong.io/blog/does-cold-email-even-work-any-more-heres-what-the-data-says), with Jason Bay of Outbound Squad; grade B). A 4–8× spread inside the same
company, same product, same list, is not a copywriting-talent spread. It is a systems spread.

### 2.1 They start from a *reason to write today*, not a template with a slot

Average teams pick a template and hunt for a personalisation token to fill. Elite teams start from an
observed change at the account — a funding event, a leadership hire, an earnings-call remark, a
regulatory deadline, a job posting that reveals a build-vs-buy decision — and only then decide whether
an email is even warranted. If there is no reason to write today, they don't write today.

This is the highest-leverage difference and it is upstream of every word choice. The measured signature:
signal-referencing emails cluster at 5–18% reply, generic sends at 1–3% (grade E — vendor benchmark,
widely repeated, not independently reproduced).

### 2.2 They mine primary buyer language instead of inventing it

Josh Braun's fourth principle — **"Use your prospect's words"** — prescribes three concrete sources: scan
customer success stories for the quotes describing *the problem that caused the switch*; interview
customers who bought in the last 45 days ("How did you know you needed to start looking for X? What did
you try that didn't work?"); and listen to inbound discovery calls
([15 Cold Email Copywriting Principles](https://joshbraun.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/15coldemail-copywriting.pdf); grade D). He recommends maintaining a standing
**"Lingo Library"** — a document of the exact phrases customers use.

The litmus test he gives is one line: **"Would a customer say this?"**

Elite teams run this as a scheduled ritual (a monthly pass over call recordings and won/lost notes),
not as an individual rep's habit. The whole premise of conversation-intelligence tooling is that this
language is a *corporate asset*, and average teams leave it on the floor.

### 2.3 They refuse to pitch in the first touch

Gong's finding is blunt: **pitching reduces reply rates by up to 57%**, and subject-line buzzwords and
numbers reduce opens by up to **17.9%** (grade B). The exec-specific study adds that emails avoiding
"AI," "platform," and feature-led positioning outperform, and that vague ROI claims measurably hurt
([Gong, "Do execs really reply to cold email?"](https://www.gong.io/blog/do-execs-really-reply-to-cold-email-here-s-what-the-data-says), analysis across 1M+ executive sales cycles, produced with
Jen Allen-Knuth and authored by Dan Morgese; grade B).

The average rep believes the email's job is to explain the product. The elite rep believes its job is
to **earn a reply**, and treats the product as something that appears in touch three, if at all.

### 2.4 They QA every send against a rubric

Lavender's own benchmark across **231,818 cold emails** found A-graded emails replied at **4.3% vs 3.4%**
for the rest — a **~27% lift** from nothing but pre-send scoring (grade B). The rubric dimensions
Lavender scores are worth stealing whether or not you buy the tool: subject-line strength, word count,
reading level, personalisation quality, CTA clarity, and tone.

The behavioural difference is that elite teams have a **gate** — a thing a draft must pass before it can
be sent — and average teams have *opinions*. §8 of this document is a rubric you can use directly.

### 2.5 They tier effort against account value, explicitly

A three-tier model is the common shape: Tier 1 fully hand-researched, Tier 2 blended (researched opening
line, templated body), Tier 3 templated with structural personalisation only. The point is not that
tiering is clever; it is that the **research budget per account is a decision someone made on purpose**,
rather than an accident of how much time the rep had on Thursday.

The Salesloft data makes the cost of *not* doing this visible: across 20 industries, personalisation
averaged 10–14% of message content, meaning **at least 86% of all sent emails contained no
personalisation at all** — while crossing a 15% threshold correlated with **2.75× the reply rate**
(3.4M-cadence sample; grade B).

### 2.6 They lower the ask, deliberately, and they know why

See §5.6. This is the one place where practitioner consensus, large-N vendor data, and peer-reviewed
social psychology all point the same direction — which is rare enough to be worth trusting.

### 2.7 They measure replies, positive replies, and meetings — never opens

Apple Mail Privacy Protection pre-fetches tracking pixels regardless of whether a human opened anything.
Open rate is now a corrupted metric, and any A/B test decided on opens is decided on noise. Elite teams
report **reply rate**, **positive reply rate**, and **meetings held**, and they know their positive-reply
rate is roughly an order of magnitude below their raw reply rate.

Calibration for 2026 (grade B/E, wide methodological variance): platform-wide averages sit near **3.4%**
reply; 5–8% is good; **10%+ is the elite threshold**; tightly-targeted segments occasionally reach
15–25%. C-level specifically runs around **6.4%** when the outreach demonstrates genuine account
knowledge.

### 2.8 They write follow-ups that add, and they stop

Every touch must carry something the previous one didn't — a different problem angle, a new artefact, a
different stakeholder's perspective. The measured penalty for the alternative is direct: **"I never heard
back" reduced meetings booked by 14%** and asking for the prospect's **"thoughts?" reduced them by 20%**
(Gong; grade B).

Braun's thirteenth principle names the failure mode precisely — an **anxious attachment style** applied
to a stranger's inbox: *"Being too attached creates what you're fearing the most."* His replacement is a
single-sentence, ego-free re-open borrowed from Chris Voss: *"Have you given up on this project?"*

Sequence shape, cadence and the disputed share-of-replies-from-follow-ups belong to
[`email-optimization.md`](email-optimization.md) §4–5; they are not re-derived here.

## 3. Structure: the anatomy that the evidence supports

### 3.1 The reader model you are writing for

Three independent findings converge on the same picture, and the structure falls out of it:

- **Nine seconds.** That is what Gong observes executives spending on an email (grade B).
- **People scan; they do not read.** Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking study (June 2006, 42 participants, 117 email newsletters) found only **19% of newsletters were read fully**, that **67% of users had zero eye fixations in the introductory text**, and that attention concentrated on **the first two words** of headings ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/email-newsletters-inbox-congestion/); grade B — old, and about newsletters, but the scanning mechanism is not email-format-specific).
- **Buyers have almost no time allocated to you.** Gartner's survey of **632 B2B buyers (Aug–Sep 2024)** found buyers spend **17% of the total buying cycle** meeting *all* potential suppliers — dropping to **5–6% per supplier** in a competitive deal — and **61% now prefer a rep-free buying experience** ([Gartner, Jun 2025](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-sales-survey-finds-61-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-buying-experience); grade C).

**Design consequence:** the first sentence must survive being the *only* sentence read, and the
introductory pleasantry is a measured dead zone. Write as if the reader will read line one and the last
line and nothing in between — because a meaningful share of them will.

### 3.2 The five-part skeleton

Every high-performing framework in the sources reduces to the same five moves in the same order. Josh
Braun's six-sentence framework (poke the bear → objection → third-party proof → explanation → ask →
easy out) and Gong's exec findings (open on their priority → peer social proof → value CTA) are the same
shape described from different angles.

| # | Element | Job | Words | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Trigger line** | Prove in one sentence that this was written *to them, today* | 10–20 | signal-anchored reply lift; social loafing (Braun #3) |
| 2 | **Problem hypothesis** | Name the expensive, frequent problem in *their* words | 15–30 | Braun #2 "solve an expensive problem", #4 "use your prospect's words"; Corporate Visions unconsidered needs |
| 3 | **Peer proof** | One named-tier, quantified, *relevant* result | 15–25 | Gong: exec emails win on "credibility through social proof" |
| 4 | **Interest CTA** | A yes/no question that costs nothing to answer | 5–12 | Gong 304,174-email CTA study (§5.6) |
| 5 | **Easy out** | Explicit permission to decline or redirect | 5–10 | Braun #13 "detach"; and see §6.3 for why this matters far more in Singapore |

**Order matters and is not arbitrary.** The trigger line must come first because it is the only thing
that buys the second sentence. The CTA must come last because it is the second-most-read position on the
page. The proof sits *between* problem and ask so that the ask is answered from a position of "this
person might actually know something," not "this person wants my calendar."

### 3.3 What to leave out

- **The greeting paragraph.** "Hope you're well" / "I hope this finds you well." NN/g: 67% zero fixations on intros. (Gong's own data contains an apparent counter-finding — "hope all is well" correlating with **+24%** meetings booked. Treat this as **contested and probably confounded**: reps who write warmly may differ systematically from reps who don't. Do not build a rule on it.)
- **Your company boilerplate.** The one-line "who we are" belongs in the signature or touch three.
- **Second and third CTAs.** One ask.
- **Anything requiring a click to understand.** The email must be self-contained; the link is optional evidence, not the payload.
- **Attachments and images in touch one.** (Reason belongs to [`email-deliverability.md`](email-deliverability.md); the craft reason is that it makes the email look like a campaign.)

## 4. Length: the honest version

This is the most-quoted and worst-evidenced parameter in cold email. Five large datasets disagree:

| Source | Recommended length | n | What was measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Lavender](https://lavender.ai/blog/best-length-cold-email) (Will Allred, Jan 2023) | **25–50 words** | "billions of emails, ~50k inboxes" | reply rate, **openers only** |
| [Gong — exec study](https://www.gong.io/blog/do-execs-really-reply-to-cold-email-here-s-what-the-data-says) | **50–100 words**; "drops sharply" past 100 | 1M+ exec sales cycles | reply rate, C-level recipients |
| [Gong — general](https://www.gong.io/blog/does-cold-email-even-work-any-more-heres-what-the-data-says) | **3–4 sentences, ≤100 words** | 28M+ emails | reply rate |
| [Gong — cold email stats](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-email-stats) | **30–150 words** "significantly outperformed shorter" | "millions" | **meetings booked** |
| [Boomerang](https://blog.boomerangapp.com/2016/02/7-tips-for-getting-more-responses-to-your-emails-with-data/) | **50–125 words** | 5.3M messages | response rate, general email |

**They are not actually in conflict — they measure different things.** Lavender's number is explicitly
for *openers* and Allred flags that Gong's longer-is-better finding applies to *follow-ups*. Gong's
"30–150 outperformed shorter" is optimised for **meetings booked**, not replies — a longer email filters
harder and converts the replies it does get. The exec-specific number is the one matched to our question.

**The operating rule that survives all five:**

- **First touch to a CxO: 50–100 words.** Under 50 you cannot fit a credible trigger + problem + proof; over 100 you are past the measured cliff.
- **Hard ceiling 125 words**, no exceptions, and treat anything over 100 as requiring justification.
- **Four sentences is the target sentence count.** Not four lines — four sentences.
- **Follow-ups may be shorter (one sentence is fine) but must never be longer than touch one.**
- **On mobile, 100 words is roughly two thumb-scrolls.** Mobile share of B2B opens is reported around 38% (grade E, wide variance) — enough that a 150-word email is a scroll for a third of your recipients before they know why you wrote.

## 5. Element-by-element

### 5.1 Subject line

**The evidence genuinely conflicts here, and the conflict is instructive.**

- Gong: open rates decline as subject lines lengthen; **1–4 words performs best**; buzzwords and numbers cut opens by up to **17.9%** (grade B, B2B sales prospecting).
- Backlinko/Pitchbox: **36–50 characters** performed best, and long subject lines beat short ones ([12M emails, Apr 2019](https://backlinko.com/email-outreach-study); grade B).

**Resolution.** The Backlinko study is (a) from 2019, before Apple MPP made opens unmeasurable and before
generative AI flooded inboxes, and (b) **not sales outreach at all** — it studied link-building, guest-post
and PR pitches to bloggers and publishers, a population with completely different inbox economics. For a
CxO prospecting email, **Gong's population is the matching one.** Note also that 36–50 characters is
roughly 5–8 words, so the two findings are closer than the headlines suggest.

**What to do:**

- **1–5 words. Lowercase. Noun phrase, not a sentence.**
- Anchor it to **their world, not your product**. Allen-Knuth's worked pattern for an education-sector executive is a subject line like *"student dropout risk"* — the executive's own agenda item, verbatim, with no vendor vocabulary in it.
- **Boring beats clever.** A subject line that reads like an internal email from a colleague is the target. Anything that reads like marketing has already lost.
- **No brackets, no emoji, no ALL CAPS, no numbers, no question marks in touch one.**
- **Optimise for reply, not open.** You cannot honestly measure opens any more, and a subject line that wins the open and loses the reply is worse than useless — it burns the account.

**On personalisation tokens in the subject line:** the strongest evidence in existence is
**A-grade** — Sahni, Wheeler & Chintagunta, *Marketing Science* 37(2) 2018, randomised field experiments
across millions of recipients with three organisations: adding the recipient's name to the subject line
raised opens from **9.05% → 10.80% (+20%)**, sales leads from **0.39% → 0.51% (+31%)**, and *reduced*
unsubscribes from **1.2% → 1.0%** ([paper](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mksc.2017.1066)).
**But** this is consumer marketing email, and the mechanism the authors identify is that *non-informative*
personal content signals effort. In 2026 B2B, a first-name token is no longer non-informative — it is the
universal tell of a mail merge. **Take the mechanism, reject the tactic:** the subject line must signal
effort, and in B2B the only content that still does that is *their specific situation*, not their name.

### 5.2 The trigger line (sentence one)

This is the whole email. If it fails, nothing after it is read.

**It must contain a fact that could not be true of any other company.** Not "I saw you're scaling" —
which is true of everyone. Something with a date, a number, a name, or a document behind it.

Braun's mechanism for why generic openers fail is **social loafing**: when people feel they have no unique
contribution to make, they feel little responsibility to act. A copy-pasted email tells the reader they
are one of a thousand, and a thousand other people can answer it.

**Anti-patterns that read as personalised but are not:**

| Looks personal | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "I saw you went to [university]" | Irrelevant to the problem; reads as scraping |
| "Congrats on the funding!" | Every vendor sent this the same week |
| "I noticed you're hiring SDRs" | True of ~40% of your list; not a *situation* |
| "Love what you're building at [Co]" | Contains zero information; costs zero effort to write |
| "As a [title], you probably..." | Persona, not person — Braun #5, "write for one" |

**The test:** delete the company name and the recipient's name. If the sentence still makes sense, it is
not personalisation, it is a template.

### 5.3 The problem hypothesis

You are not asking a discovery question. You are **stating a hypothesis about their situation that they
can cheaply confirm or deny.** That asymmetry is what makes it answerable in nine seconds.

Two named frameworks converge here:

**Braun's "poke the bear."** A *neutral* question — not a leading one — that makes the reader examine
their current approach. His worked example: *"How are you identifying unused or redundant software?"* The
psychology he cites is **loss aversion**: illuminating what they stand to lose by doing nothing is roughly
twice as motivating as an equivalent gain. He also insists the problem must be **intense and frequent**
to earn attention — his "dead pixel" test: a real problem that isn't expensive enough gets ignored, and
most cold emails are about dead pixels.

**Corporate Visions' "unconsidered needs."** Tim Riesterer's research with Stanford social psychologist
Dr. Zakary Tormala — a **400-participant B2B simulation** — found that messaging introducing a need the
buyer had not considered outperformed both stated-needs response and "value-added" messaging: **+11%**
on presentation quality, **+41%** on uniqueness, **+10%** on attitude and purchase intent
([Corporate Visions](https://corporatevisions.com/blog/unconsidered-needs/); grade B, closest thing here
to a controlled experiment). The mechanism Tormala gives: an unconsidered need is *unexpected*, which
creates uncertainty, which accelerates decision processing.

**The critical scope condition, and it is the most commonly-violated rule in this document:** this applies
**only when you are attacking the status quo**. Riesterer's own finding is that with existing customers
you must do the exact opposite and *reinforce* their status quo. Do not run a "why change" disruption play
on a renewal or an expansion.

**Writing rules that follow:**
- Use their vocabulary, not yours. Braun: *"Would a customer say this?"*
- Prefer **cost of inaction** to benefit of action.
- Name **one** problem. A list of three problems reads as a brochure and lets the reader deny all three at once.

### 5.4 Peer proof

**One** proof point. Named tier, quantified, and structurally relevant to the recipient — same industry,
same size, same regulatory environment, or same problem. Gong's exec finding is that emails
"reinforce credibility through social proof" outperform, and that **vague ROI claims measurably reduce
replies** — so an unquantified or unattributable claim is worse than no claim.

Braun's construction for the outcome is the **"without" formula**: *(desired result) without (the thing
that sucks)* — *"grew to $7M ARR without hiring salespeople, cold calling, or running ads."* The "without"
is where the differentiation lives, because the reader is already achieving the desired result some other,
more painful way. His related test is **"be meaningfully different"**: what can they now do because of X
that they cannot do without it?

**Pitfalls:** logo walls (unfalsifiable); "companies like yours" (nobody believes it); percentages with no
base ("300% improvement" in what, from what); and claiming a customer you cannot name — an unnameable
reference is not proof, it is a rumour.

### 5.5 Reading level, sentence length and rhythm

Boomerang's analysis of **5.3M messages**: emails written at a **third-grade reading level got 36% more
responses than college-level** emails and **17% more than high-school level**; including **1–3 questions
raised response rate by 50%** (grade B). Lavender scores toward **grade 3–5** on the same logic.

This is not about the reader's intelligence. It is about **processing cost under a nine-second budget**,
and it is exactly why jargon fails: Braun's tenth principle is that jargon makes you "a vanilla scoop in a
sea of vanilla scoops," and his fix is Robert Collier's rule — *join the conversation already happening in
their mind.*

**Mechanical rules:**
- **One idea per sentence.** Average sentence length under 15 words.
- **Delete `just`, `really`, `actually`, `simply`, `very`, `that`.** Braun's sixth principle is an editing pass whose only job is deletion; Stephen King's rule of cutting 10% is the reference.
- **Read it aloud.** If you would not say it to a stranger at a conference, do not send it.
- **Ask exactly one question**, and make it the CTA.
- **No unglossed acronym or technical term** that the recipient's own team would not use in a meeting.

### 5.6 The CTA — the best-evidenced rule in cold email

**The finding.** Gong analysed **304,174 emails** and found that at the cold stage, **interest-based CTAs**
("is this worth exploring?") substantially outperform meeting requests, while **specific time-bound CTAs
win later**, at the active-deal stage. Restatements of the same study report the cold-stage lift as
anywhere from **2×** to **15% vs 30%** booking rate — the *numbers are unreliable, the direction is not.*
Gong's exec-specific work sharpens it further: for C-level, the best-performing CTA is often not a
question at all but a **value offer** — the example given is offering a pre-built benchmark showing how
the executive's institution compares to peers.

**Why it works — and this is the part worth internalising, because it comes from outside sales
entirely.** Roghanizad & Bohns, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 69 (2017), pp. 223–226,
"Ask in person: You're less persuasive than you think over email" — **grade A, n=975.** Across two
studies, requesters *underestimated* compliance for face-to-face asks (replicating prior work) but
**overestimated** it over email. The mechanism the authors identify: requesters, anchored on their own
perspective, **fail to appreciate how untrustworthy their email looks to a stranger.** The HBR framing
of the effect size is that an in-person ask is roughly **34× more effective** than the same ask by email.

The implication is precise and it is not "email doesn't work." It is that **email conveys far less trust
per unit of ask than you believe it does, so the ask must be priced accordingly.** A 30-minute calendar
request is a large ask made through the lowest-trust channel available. "Worth exploring?" is a small ask
made through the same channel — correctly priced.

**Construction rules:**
- **One CTA. Last line.**
- **Yes/no answerable.** The reply you are optimising for is one word long.
- **No calendar link in touch one.** No "does Tuesday 3pm or Thursday 10am work?" — that question presumes a decision they have not made.
- Braun's ninth principle states the friction argument directly: *"Don't ask people for 30 minutes of their time. That's like asking someone you just met if you could meet their parents."*
- **The value-offer variant is stronger for CxOs than the interest question** where you genuinely have an artefact (a benchmark, a peer teardown, a one-page analysis) that costs them nothing to receive.
- **Pair it with the easy out.** *"If this isn't a priority, just say so and I'll close the loop."* Braun folds the "easy out" into his six-sentence framework as a distinct element, and §6.3 explains why it is not optional for Singapore.

### 5.7 Sign-off and signature

- **Plain text. No banner image, no marketing tagline, no pronoun-heavy block, no "Sent from" cruft.**
- Name, title, company, one link maximum.
- **The signature is where your company boilerplate goes** — one clause, not a paragraph. This keeps the body about them.
- **Match the salutation register to the market** (§6).

## 6. Tone for CxOs: United States vs. Singapore

### 6.0 How to use this section

Country-level cultural data describes **priors, not people.** The Culture Factor's own guidance is
explicit: *"differences among the values of individuals in one country tend to be bigger than the value
differences between countries."* Singapore in particular is small, multi-ethnic and intensely
internationalised; a large share of Singapore-based CxOs are Western-educated, expatriate, or running
regional roles for US-headquartered firms.

**So the rule is: use the prior only until you have evidence about the individual.** Their published
writing, their LinkedIn register, how they signed their last email, whether their company's comms are
formal or casual — all of these outrank the country score. What the prior is genuinely good for is
**deciding which way to err when you have nothing.** And the asymmetry there is stable: the cost of being
slightly too formal in Singapore is near zero; the cost of being too familiar is a silent delete.

### 6.1 The measured difference

Scores from [The Culture Factor / Hofstede country comparison tool](https://www.theculturefactor.com/country-comparison-tool) — **note these are the revised
Minkov–Hofstede figures, last updated 16 October 2023 for Individualism and Long-Term Orientation.**
Most blog posts still quote the pre-2023 numbers (Singapore IDV 20, US IDV 91). **Those are stale.**

| Dimension | Singapore | United States | Delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power Distance | **74** | **40** | **+34** — the big one |
| Individualism | 43 | 60 | +17 US |
| Motivation toward Achievement & Success | 48 | 62 | +14 US |
| Uncertainty Avoidance | **8** | 46 | **−38 SG** — counter-intuitive, see 6.5 |
| Long-Term Orientation | 67 | 50 | +17 SG |
| Indulgence | 46 | 68 | +22 US |

The commentary attached to those scores is as useful as the numbers. For **Singapore**: *"Power is
centralised… communication is indirect with selective information flow"*; *"everyone's 'face' — dignity,
self-respect and prestige — is protected"*; *"a 'yes' need not mean yes, politeness takes precedence over
blunt feedback, and relationships come before the task"*; and on the achievement dimension, *"modesty and
humility matter, so acting as if one knows it all is disliked."*

For the **United States**: *"hierarchy is established for convenience, superiors are always accessible…
communication is informal, direct and participative"*; and *"Americans will tend to display and talk
freely about their 'successes'… being successful per se is not a great motivator, but being able to show
one's success."*

Erin Meyer's Culture Map places the US at the extreme **low-context** end of the communicating scale, with
Singapore meaningfully further toward high-context — though notably **less so than Japan, Korea or China**,
which is consistent with Singapore's British-inflected institutional and legal culture. Australia's SBS
Cultural Atlas adds the operational detail: Singaporean counterparts *"seldom give a flat negative
response,"* workplaces are *"hierarchical based on age and position,"* meetings are *"formal, reserved and
slow moving,"* and partners *"prefer to cultivate partnerships that will last as opposed to sealing a
quick deal."*

### 6.2 What actually changes in the email

**What does NOT change:** length, the five-part structure, one idea, one CTA, no pitch, reading level,
peer proof. Those are cognitive-load facts, not cultural ones. **Do not add words to add formality.**

**What DOES change — six things:**

| Element | US CxO | Singapore CxO |
|---|---|---|
| **Salutation** | `Hi [First],` — first name is the default and formality reads as distance | `Dear Ms [Surname],` on first touch. Migrate to first name only after they do. |
| **Sentence one** | The point, immediately. A warm-up line is a tax on a low-context reader. | The point, immediately — **but framed as an observation, not an assertion about their business.** |
| **Credibility source** | Personal + outcome: what you've done, what it produced | **Institutional:** who else you work with, named peer firms, a regulator/standard/analyst reference. High power distance means a junior title asserting peer standing lands badly. |
| **Register of proof** | Confident, quantified, comfortably assertive | Quantified but **understated**. "Acting as if one knows it all is disliked." Drop superlatives entirely. |
| **The out** | Nice to have | **Load-bearing.** See 6.3. |
| **Forwardability** | Useful | **Structural requirement.** See 6.4. |

**Also, mechanically:**
- **British spelling.** Singapore uses British English — *organise, programme, centre, prioritise, licence*. It is not an error to write American English, but British spelling reads local and costs you nothing.
- **Send in SGT business hours.** An email arriving at 03:00 local is visibly automated.
- **Titles are used.** If someone holds a doctorate or a formal appointment, use it on first contact.

### 6.3 Names — the highest-frequency own-goal

Getting a name wrong is the fastest way to prove your email is a mail merge, and Singapore's naming
conventions make this far easier to get wrong than in the US.

- **Chinese Singaporean names** may be written **surname-first** (Tan Wei Ling → *Ms Tan*) **or** with an adopted Western given name first (Sarah Tan Wei Ling → *Ms Tan*). In both cases the family name is **Tan**. A merge field that grabs "the first word" produces *"Dear Ms Wei"* or *"Dear Tan."*
- **Malay names** typically use patronymics rather than family surnames — *bin* (son of) / *binte*/*binti* (daughter of). The element after *bin/binti* is the **father's** name, not a surname. Address by the personal name with the honorific.
- **Indian Singaporean names** may use *s/o* (son of) or *d/o* (daughter of) on the same pattern.

**The operating rule:** if you are not certain which element is the family name, either use the **full name
as the person themselves displays it**, or — the safer default — **write the greeting by hand for every
Tier-1 Singapore account.** A CxO list is small enough that this is a rounding error in effort and a
material difference in signal.

### 6.4 "A yes need not mean yes" — designing for a high-face culture

This is the single most consequential difference and it changes what a **reply** means to you.

In a culture where face preservation outranks bluntness, a polite non-committal reply is the **default
courteous response to an unwanted email.** "Thanks for reaching out, let me look into this" is not a
buying signal. Teams that measure raw reply rate across both markets and conclude Singapore is
outperforming are frequently measuring politeness.

**Three design consequences:**

1. **Make the CTA discriminating.** An interest question ("worth exploring?") invites a courteous yes. Add a qualifier that makes a yes cost something and therefore mean something: *"Worth a look, or is this already handled?"* The second branch gives a face-preserving route to "no" that is *informative*.
2. **The easy out stops being optional.** *"If this isn't a priority right now, just tell me and I'll stop."* In the US this raises reply rate. In Singapore it is the **only reliable way to extract a true negative** — you are explicitly granting permission to decline without loss of face, which is otherwise unavailable.
3. **Re-qualify positives before you count them.** Treat an SG "yes" as unconfirmed until it survives a second, more specific question. Adjust your positive-reply definition, not your reply target.

### 6.5 Two priors most teams get backwards

**Backwards prior #1: "APAC buyers are risk-averse, so lead with safety."**
**Singapore's Uncertainty Avoidance score is 8 — among the lowest in the world**, against 46 for the US.
The Culture Factor's own commentary is that Singaporeans follow rules *because rules come from authority*
(a power-distance effect), not from a need for structure, and that there is *"real comfort with ambiguity
and a pragmatic, adaptable approach."* Combined with **Long-Term Orientation 67 vs the US's 50**, this
means: a **pilot / first-step framing works well**, a **long-horizon framing is credible** in a way it
often isn't with a quarterly-measured US buyer, and **loading the email with risk-mitigation reassurance
is solving a problem that isn't there.** What high power distance actually requires is not safety — it is
**sanctioned authority**: who endorses this, who else at your level has done it.

**Backwards prior #2: "Formality means length and flourish."**
It does not. Formality lives in **salutation, title use, absence of slang and contractions, and
understatement** — all of which are free. Every extra word still costs you against a nine-second budget.
A formal Singapore email and a casual US email should have **the same word count.**

### 6.6 Two worked examples

Same account, same trigger, same product, same length. Only register changes. *(Both fictional.)*

**US — 71 words**

> **Subject:** freight audit backlog
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> Your Q2 call mentioned invoice audit moving from quarterly to monthly. Most ops teams that make that
> switch find the audit headcount, not the software, becomes the constraint by month three.
>
> Meridian Freightways hit the same wall and cleared it without adding auditors — they now close the
> monthly cycle in four days.
>
> Worth exploring, or is this already handled?
>
> If not a priority, say so and I'll close the loop.
>
> — Alex

**Singapore — 74 words**

> **Subject:** monthly audit cycle
>
> Dear Ms Chan,
>
> I noted from Straits Meridian's Q2 results briefing that invoice audit is moving to a monthly cycle.
> In several regional logistics operators we work with, audit headcount — not the system — became the
> constraint by the third month.
>
> One APAC operator of similar scale now closes the monthly cycle in four days without additional
> auditors. I would be glad to share how.
>
> Is this something your team is already addressing?
>
> If it is not a priority, do let me know and I will not follow up.
>
> Kind regards,
> Alex

**The deltas, itemised:** `Hi Dana` → `Dear Ms Chan` · "Your Q2 call mentioned" (assertion) →
"I noted from the results briefing" (observation) · "Most ops teams" (generic authority) → "several
regional logistics operators we work with" (institutional, regional) · named peer →
unnamed-but-scaled peer (understatement) · "Worth exploring?" (invites courteous yes) → "already
addressing?" (discriminating) · "say so and I'll close the loop" → "do let me know and I will not follow
up" (no contractions, explicit permission) · `— Alex` → `Kind regards, Alex`.

## 7. Pitfalls, ranked by strength of evidence

**Tier 1 — measured, large-N, and expensive**

| Pitfall | Measured cost | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Pitching in touch one** | up to **−57%** reply rate | Gong, 28M+ (B) |
| **Generic / no signal** | 1–3% vs 5–18% reply | vendor benchmarks (E) — direction robust |
| **Over 100 words to a CxO** | "drops sharply" | Gong exec (B) |
| **College-level prose** | **−36%** vs grade-3 level | Boomerang, 5.3M (B) |
| **Asking for 30 minutes in touch one** | interest CTA outperforms at cold stage | Gong, 304,174 (B) + Bohns 2017 (A) |
| **"Thoughts?" as the ask** | **−20%** meetings booked | Gong (B) |
| **"I never heard back" in a follow-up** | **−14%** meetings booked | Gong (B) |
| **Vague ROI language** | reported **−15%** and **−17%** in different restatements | Gong (B) — *flagged: inconsistent* |
| **Buzzwords/numbers in subject** | up to **−17.9%** opens | Gong (B) |

**Tier 2 — strong mechanism, weak numbers**

- **Fake personalisation.** A token where research should be. Fails Braun's social-loafing test; fails the delete-the-name test (§5.2).
- **Two or more CTAs.** Splits the decision; the reader defers, and deferral is a delete.
- **Persona-writing.** "As a CFO, you're probably focused on…" — Braun #5, *write for one*.
- **Mentioning AI.** One restatement of the Gong/30MPC/Jason Bay 85M-email collaboration reports a **−36%** reply rate for mentioning AI. **Grade E, single unverified restatement — do not quote the number.** The direction is consistent with the buzzword finding and with the fact that "AI" is now inbox wallpaper.
- **Fully AI-generated copy, unedited.** Reported at ~1 percentage point lower reply and ~3× the spam-flag rate (grade E). Treat the number as unreliable; treat the mechanism as obvious — homogeneous copy at volume is *detectable by the reader*, not just the filter.
- **The status-quo mismatch.** Running "why change" disruption on an existing customer. Riesterer's research says explicitly to do the opposite (§5.3).

**Tier 3 — structural, no data needed**

- Sending to a badly-qualified list. The copy ceiling is set by the list; no sentence rescues the wrong recipient. (Owner: [`email-optimization.md`](email-optimization.md) §12.)
- Judging anything by open rate (Apple MPP).
- Declaring a winner from an A/B test with n=200. At a 3% base rate, detecting a 1-point difference needs thousands per arm. Most "our test showed…" claims in this field are noise.
- Getting the recipient's name structurally wrong (§6.3).

## 8. Pre-send rubric

Ten binary checks. **Any "no" is a rewrite, not a tweak.**

1. **Trigger:** does sentence one contain a fact that could not be true of another company? *(Delete the company name — does it still make sense? Then it fails.)*
2. **One idea:** can you state the email's single point in six words?
3. **Word count:** 50–100? (hard ceiling 125)
4. **Sentences:** four or fewer?
5. **Reading level:** grade 3–5? No unglossed jargon or acronyms?
6. **No pitch:** zero product features, zero category nouns, zero "we help companies…"?
7. **Proof:** exactly one, quantified, structurally relevant, and attributable?
8. **CTA:** exactly one, last line, yes/no answerable, no calendar link, no time slots?
9. **Out:** is declining explicitly permitted and costless?
10. **Register:** salutation, spelling, name handling and formality matched to market (§6)? Would a customer say these words out loud?

**Then read it aloud.** Braun's rule — two passes, one minute apart, deleting on each — catches more than
any checklist.
