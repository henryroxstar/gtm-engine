# Interview protocol — finding the story before verifying it

> **Company-neutral.** This is the *mechanism* for getting a true story out of a customer
> conversation. It carries no company facts. Who to interview, and which seat the story is
> written for, come from Step 1 of the skill; what the story may claim comes from Step 5's
> ledger. This file only governs the questions.

## Why a protocol, and why this one

Ask a customer for "a story" and you get a polished success anecdote: they wanted a thing,
they did the thing, it worked. It reads well, it verifies cleanly, and it is not a story —
it is an accomplishment. Every reader recognises the shape and discounts it.

Ask instead for the moment they **doubted themselves most**, and they take you to the
low point — which is where a story is actually born. The low point is the beacon. It is
the moment before the decision that changed the outcome, and it carries the details a
brainstorm cannot invent: the wrong first call, the reason it was made, what it cost, what
they saw when they looked at it honestly.

This matters to the skill's own rules. Step 6 rule 5 — *what they tried first, and why it
failed* — is "the single highest-credibility passage in the document and the most
commonly omitted." It is omitted because nobody asked for it. The protocol asks for it
first.

## When to run it

At Step 1, whenever a live conversation with the customer (or the champion inside the
customer) is available — a call, a recorded meeting, a written exchange. Run it **before**
Step 4's research, so research verifies a story that exists rather than assembling one
from materials.

When no conversation is available, skip it, and say in the report that decision 1 and the
low point were **inferred from materials**. That is a legitimate state; pretending an
inferred low point was told to you is not.

## The five questions — in this order

Ask them in order. Each one's answer is what makes the next one askable.

1. **When, in this whole effort, did you doubt yourselves the most?**
   *Locates the low point.* Listen for the feeling, not the event — the feeling should be
   the opposite of whatever value the finished story will stand for (control → helplessness;
   confidence → exposure; belonging → isolation).

2. **What decision did you doubt most in that moment?**
   *Uncovers decision 1* — the first call, made for reasons that looked sound at the time.
   This is Step 6 rule 5's passage, told by the person who made it.

3. **Why did you make that decision — what were you hoping it would get you?**
   *The want.* The stated goal. Usually a number, a deadline, a thing a board or a boss
   asked for.

4. **What was the real reason you wanted that?**
   *The need behind the want.* The honest answer is one layer down — a fear of looking
   behind, a promise already made, a reputation on the line. This is the line that makes
   the quote in structure §9 sound like a person instead of a press release. It is also
   the answer most likely to be off the record; ask, then respect the answer.

5. **What did you realise that let you make the decision that got you out — and what was
   that decision?**
   *Decision 2, and the meaning.* The insight is the point of the story; the decision is
   what the document's solution section describes.

## Follow-ups, only after the five

These fill in scenes. They come naturally once the spine exists, and they are useless
before it does:

- What triggered decision 1 — what happened, from outside, that made it urgent?
- Which kinds of pressure followed it: the environment working against you (cost, time,
  the market); people working against you (a vendor, a committee, a regulator); doubt
  working against you (the internal one)? In what order?
- Where were you when the low point landed? Who was with you? What did you do next?

## The classifier — re-run at Step 8

Every customer story is one of three things. Name which, before calling the draft done:

| kind | shape | what it earns |
|---|---|---|
| **Anecdote** | things happened; no decision, no low point, no meaning | nothing — pleasant, forgettable |
| **Accomplishment** | want → act → succeed, no real obstacle | *resentment* — the reader recognises a brag in narrative form and discounts everything after it |
| **True story** | decision 1 → mounting pressure → the low point → decision 2 → the outcome, which carries the meaning | the reader's trust, because it is the shape their own work has |

The test is mechanical: **point at decision 1, the low point, and decision 2 in the draft.**
If any of the three has no sentence, the draft is an accomplishment story. Say so in the
report. Do not ship it under a title that promises a story it does not contain.

## The pressure that removes the story, named

The reason accomplishment stories are the majority is not ignorance; it is a specific,
predictable pressure: the belief that the brand must always be shown in a positive light,
so any struggle, wrong turn or doubt must be cut. It comes from the customer's marketing
or comms team at approval time, and it arrives as reasonable-sounding notes — *soften
this*, *do we need to say it failed first*, *can this be about the solution*.

Name it when it happens. The skill's own rule already holds the line — rule 7, *concede
your own limits, never the customer's* — and the answer to the note is the same every
time: the wrong first call is what makes the second one credible. A story with the
struggle removed is not safer. It is boring, and the reader stops before the outcome.

## What this protocol is not

- **Not a script to read aloud.** Five questions, in order, in the interviewer's own words.
- **Not permission to invent.** A low point that was not told to you is inferred, and the
  report says so. The protocol exists precisely because invented low points read as
  manufactured — the melodrama every reader has learned to spot.
- **Not a substitute for Step 4.** The story is found here and verified there. A vivid
  low point with an unverifiable number attached to it is still an unverifiable number.
